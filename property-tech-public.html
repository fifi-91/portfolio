The challenge

People wanted to own a home. The journey got in the way.

Saudi homeownership rose from about 47% in 2016 to 66% in 2025, on the way to a Vision 2030 target of 70%. Demand was there. But finding, financing and living in a home happened across sales centres, banks, paperwork and separate service channels. Turning a lead into a sale was slow.

Decisions I owned

  • One system for three products, not three systems. Products differ in colour and tone, never in how things work.
  • Arabic from the first component, not a translation pass at the end. Every component has a language property.
  • Tokens and names as the contract with engineering, so a Figma component and its Storybook counterpart are the same thing.
  • Build the client’s capability, so their team could run and extend the system after we left.

From journey to a scoped first release

I mapped every user activity in Home Services, from opening the mini app to getting help afterwards. Then I traced each one through to an MVP requirement and a screen. The first release stopped at a coherent core: request, book, confirm, track and give feedback. Everything else was staged for later.

Payment waited.

New homes were under a two-year warranty, so service requests were free at launch. In-app payment could follow without blocking the first release.

UX first, then backend scale.

Version 1 had to make booking feel effortless. Version 2 was where scalable backend capabilities came in.

Design system

From designing pages to building a system.

When I arrived, the team designed each page on its own. With three products, two languages and several squads, that meant repeated work and inconsistent builds. I introduced an atomic design system, based on Brad Frost’s model, so we designed reusable parts once, from atoms up to full templates, and assembled pages from them in both languages.

A business case, not just a Figma library

A system changes how an organisation works. I made the case for it to senior stakeholders on both the consultancy and client sides. The argument rested on speed, reuse and ownership, held together by governance.

The solution

One bilingual system, from first visit to signed agreement.

We built 3,000+ components. 2,695 were ready for launch and 1,500+ are in use across the three products. Counting variants, the library holds 5,066, including 1,838 in Arabic, with light and dark modes. Here is the home acquisition journey it made possible, and a prototype you can try in both languages.

Arabic as a primary script, not a mirrored copy

Flipping the layout is the easy part. The decisions that matter are about how Arabic reads. We chose a typeface designed for Arabic, rather than a Latin font with an Arabic fallback, and gave Arabic its own typographic rules. Then we decided element by element what mirrors and what doesn’t. You can see these rules working in the prototype below.

The design decisions behind the numbers

These results belong to the whole programme. These are the design decisions most directly connected to them.

46 → 3 days: one journey, not seven channels.

A buyer can browse, reserve, e-sign and pay the reservation fee in one app. That replaced the sales centre visit, bank appointment and paper forms shown at the top of this page.

15× conversion: fewer reasons to stop.

The financial calculator shows buyers what they can afford before they commit. The three-step reservation always shows what comes next. Both were designed to stop a qualified lead dropping out between interest and reservation.

Releases every two weeks: the system.

Shared components named the same in Figma and Storybook got the first release out in under five months and kept releases coming every two weeks.

Two languages without twice the work.

Every component was built for English and Arabic from the start. Arabic never had to wait for a separate translation pass before it could launch.

What I’d do differently

  • Start the handover on day one. The client’s designers were coached as the programme scaled. If they had been involved from the first component, they would have helped shape the system, not just inherited it.
  • Measure design decisions, not only the funnel. The team logged 100+ events per session to track user behaviour and the sales funnel. I’d connect specific design changes to those events earlier, so the impact of design shows up on its own.
  • Delegate whole problems, not screens. With three interns and no other senior designer, I learned to give each of them a whole problem rather than screens. Next time I’d do that from day one, so I could focus on the system and stakeholders sooner.
